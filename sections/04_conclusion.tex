\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Requiring capital to use AI capability changes the growth mechanism, but it does not have a uniform stabilizing effect. With one freely reallocable stock, scarcity is imposed once through $K=K_Y+K_X+K_R$. Capability raises the productivity of the capital assigned to inference and research; it never appears as a stand-alone producer. This is the minimal architecture in which the economic bottleneck can be distinguished from an accounting duplication.

The elasticity of substitution between AI services and effective labor determines whether that bottleneck binds asymptotically. Under gross complementarity, a regular infinite-horizon equilibrium is labor-anchored. Output, aggregate capital, consumption, and AI services grow with effective labor. Capability and the two computational allocations grow at different rates, and the fractions of capital used in inference and research vanish. This is not a full positive-share BGP, although its macroeconomic aggregates are asymptotically balanced.

At unit elasticity, the model admits an exact equilibrium BGP when the feedback is subcritical, $\eta+\omega_X<1$, and the stated curvature restrictions hold. The proof constructs the compatible initial levels and verifies both transversality conditions. If the threshold fails, no positive finite-rate BGP can satisfy the necessary growth equations; the supercritical reduced system has an explosive direction, but a separate global argument is needed to classify all transition paths.

Under gross substitutability, capital ceases to be a long-run ceiling. A regular bounded-capability tail contradicts the developer's costate equation. On a regular AI-dominated branch, output per unit of capital is proportional to $B^{1-\alpha}$, investment and research retain positive limiting shares, and $\dot z$ becomes proportional to $z^2$. The resulting singularity occurs in finite time. Depreciation and discounting affect lower-order terms but cannot offset that asymptotic feedback.

The substitute result is deliberately narrower than a claim of general nonexistence. The singular branch is not an infinite-horizon equilibrium, and the analysis does not rule out irregular, oscillatory, or boundary continuations. Establishing whether such paths exist---and, if they do, whether they satisfy global optimality and transversality---is the remaining theoretical question. Sector-specific capital, adjustment costs, and human research labor should be introduced only after that question is settled for the one-stock benchmark.
